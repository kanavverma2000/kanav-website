If you own one

You own a wind farm, and it earned less than the market price.

Everyone in your region got the average. You got less than that, and the board wants to know why. So: how much of the gap was the grid switching you off, and how much was you choosing not to sell into a price you didn't like? Those are two completely different problems with two completely different fixes, and almost nothing on the market separates them for you. This does, per unit, per month, measured against your own unconstrained forecast rather than against a regional average.

Rests on: curtailment split by cause, economic against network, over 217 semi‑scheduled units. Per‑unit capture at five‑minute resolution across 22.3M records. Limit: the split is attributed over the capped subset, 7.60 of 8.37 TWh in 2025. The rest stays unattributed rather than guessed.
If you're choosing where

You're deciding where to build, and the brochure only shows you good years.

A resource assessment built on ten years of wind has never seen the year that decides whether your project works. Thirty‑six years of hourly wind and sun, rebuilt for 286 projects and 50,168 MW, means the bad year is in the sample instead of averaged out of it. The traces are calibrated to what units were forecast to be able to generate, not to what they actually sent out, because metered output is already depressed by curtailment and calibrating to it quietly bakes the curtailment into your resource.

Rests on: hourly wind and solar, 1990 to 2025, calibrated to unconstrained forecast. Limit: calibration is regional, not per‑site. Five scalars serve 286 projects and between‑site variation runs 0.935 to 1.331. Ask me for the number on your site before you rely on it.
If you have to defend it

You have to defend a number to people whose job is to push back on it.

An investment committee, a counterparty, a regulator, a court. A single figure with a confident tone does not survive that room. What survives is a figure that arrives as a range, with the method attached and the weak point named before anyone else finds it. Every counterfactual here comes as two bounds: re‑clear the market at what it costs to run, and re‑clear it at what participants actually offered. The gap between those two is bidding conduct rather than physics, and naming it as such is a decomposition a single price number structurally cannot give you.

Rests on: 169 units priced from AEMO's own heat rates, fuel prices and variable O&M, each carrying its source cell. A 227‑unit offer stack. Limit: the conduct bound's level is unvalidated, $43.77 modelled against $94.84 observed, correlation 0.415. Only the mean is readable, and it is published as a range for exactly that reason.
If you have to explain it

You're writing about a closure, and everyone is quoting a different number.

Every coal closure produces a spread of confident estimates and no way to choose between them, because most of them are a rule of thumb wearing a decimal point. Here the closure is re‑cleared through the same engine that reproduces the market it is changing, for 45 units and 21.1 GW of announced retirements, and it comes back with both bounds rather than one headline. Hazelwood is in there as the worked example, because it already happened and can therefore be checked.

Rests on: coal exit module plus the counterfactual engine. Limit: announced closure dates are policy statements, not physics, and they have moved before. I report the schedule and model sensitivity to it. I do not forecast whether a date holds.
If you're just curious

You just want to know why your power bill does what it does.

Start with the game on the front page. Ten minutes, no jargon, and you have to keep the lights on for a day using five fuels. Most people fail the first time on the same thing: the sun clocks off about an hour before demand peaks. Once you have felt that once, most of the commentary about the energy transition reads differently, including the confident parts.

Rests on: a capacity‑planning model on a 32 GW system, cleared in genuine merit order. Limit: no transmission and no bidding, which are the two things that make the real market hard. The expander under the game says so.
And if none of them fit

Four people I'd send
somewhere else.

Saying who something isn't for is the cheapest way to prove the rest is honest.

  • You need to know where prices are going. I don't sell a price forecast. My own spike classifier beats a naive rule by 1.1%, which is why it isn't a product. The part that survives is the bias correction on the market operator's own pre‑dispatch, and that is a much smaller claim.
  • You need a forward loss factor for your site. I don't forecast marginal loss factors, and established products already sell that curve. Buy it from them.
  • You need to act on something right now, today. Intraday monitoring is a solved problem with good tools in it. Offers are published next day, so anything I called "live" would be a lie.
  • You need Western Australia. This is the National Electricity Market only.
