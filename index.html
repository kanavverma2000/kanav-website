Ten of ten. Year-on-year matches too: the NEM time-weighted average fell $66.06 against AEMO's stated −$66 (−47%).

Why this is harder than it looks AEMO's headline price is a time-weighted average over five-minute dispatch periods — not demand-weighted. Its separate volume-weighted series weights regional prices by initial supply plus total intermittent generation, not by total demand. Substituting total demand — the obvious guess — runs $1–2/MWh high in every region. That single wrong choice silently invalidates any capture-price benchmark built on it.
What we found

Six results the market
hasn't priced in.

Gas — displacement

Midday gas generation rose while evening gas collapsed.

Gas fell 30% over the year. AEMO reports the evening-peak concentration and names batteries as the cause. What it does not report is that midday gas output rose 30 GWh as battery charging added load — and that total sent-out energy was flat at +0.5%. Weather is a level effect; it cannot cut evening gas and raise midday gas at the same time.

Curtailment — cause

Curtailment grew five-fold. Network constraints didn't.

Network curtailment has been flat near 1 TWh a year since 2021. Economic curtailment — the plant choosing not to generate — is up 420%. In 2025, 85% of curtailed renewable energy was a price decision, not a grid limit, which inverts the usual transmission-build conclusion and moves the risk from where you connect to what gets built around you.

Capture — divergence

The best and worst assets in the NEM share a price region.

Coopers Gap Wind captured 106.6% of the Queensland average price in 2025. Darling Downs Solar, same region, captured 12.9% — an eight-fold spread inside one settlement price, about 100 km apart. Fleet medians: wind 72.1%, solar 39.8%. No regional price curve tells you which one you are buying.

Counterfactual — two bounds

A coal closure is worth $1/MWh or $20/MWh. Both are true.

Re-clearing the market at what plant costs gives ~$1–2/MWh. Re-clearing at what participants actually bid gives $14–21/MWh. The gap between the bounds is the markup component — the part that comes from conduct rather than physics. A single number structurally cannot show you that.

Supply curve — structure

The bid stack barely moves. That's why prices swing.

The offer ladder is identical to the previous trading day in 92.8% of unit-days, and band volumes are unchanged in 98.6% of consecutive intervals. But the curve is violently non-linear: median step between used bands $0, maximum $15,795. Since 2009 the cheap middle has thinned 61% while the must-run floor more than doubled.

Adequacy — coincidence

The worst weather year is not the worst reliability year.

Across 36 reconstructed weather years, 1992 has the worst mean resource in the record and produces zero unserved energy. 2009 is mid-pack on average and breaches the reliability standard 25-fold, because it holds the third-deepest 72-hour renewable drought. Sampling by average finds the dangerous year only by luck.

  • Per-unit curtailment with an economic-versus-network cause split
  • Merit-order counterfactual clearing, cost and conduct bounds published separately
  • Per-unit revenue capture at five-minute granularity, against the correct time-weighted denominator
  • Closure impact from per-unit costs traced to AEMO's own workbook cells

  • Battery dispatch, cycling and event-based valuation

What ARIA does not claim

  • No AI price forecasting — the scenario engine is an explicit merit-order clear with published assumptions
  • No forward loss-factor product — Cornwall Insight and Aurora already sell one
  • No spike-prediction edge — ours beats a naive rule by 1.1%, so it isn't sold
  • No real-time monitoring — ez2view and gridIQ are the tools for that
  • No automated bidding — running an optimiser and benchmarking one is a conflict
  • No Western Australian coverage
  • No interval-level accuracy on the conduct clear — only the mean is readable
