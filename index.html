Where these numbers come from

The demand shape, the capacity factors and the costs come from a capacity-planning model, on a system peaking at 32 GW. Solar and wind follow real hourly profiles, which is why wind blows hardest overnight and solar does nothing after six.

The clearing is genuine merit order: free renewables first, then whatever is in the battery, then coal, then gas. Cost is capital annualised over 25 years at 8% plus fuel, divided by the energy actually delivered. That last part is why parking 40 GW of anything is never the free win it looks like.

Coal carries its real constraint: it will not drop below 45% of its capacity, because in life you cannot idle a coal unit down to nothing and get it back in a hurry. Build coal and solar together and the coal simply will not move out of the way at midday, so your free energy gets thrown away. That is not a quirk of the game, it is the single biggest reason the actual grid is hard.

Two things the real market has that this doesn't: transmission between regions, and generators choosing what price to bid. The second one is most of my actual work.

If you want the answer: the cheapest mix that keeps the lights on across an average day lands near $104/MWh, and it is a balanced one, heavy on wind and solar with a little coal and a battery. The cheapest mix that also survives a still, cloudy evening costs about $110. That gap is the price of insurance, and arguing over it is roughly the entire Australian energy debate.

What the game leaves out
  • Bidding. Real generators choose a price. That choice is worth billions, and measuring it is most of what I do.
  • Ramp rates. Coal here has a floor but no speed limit. The real thing takes hours to move.
  • Transmission. The power has to physically get there, and sometimes it can't.
  • Weather that isn't average. One still, cloudy evening in a bad week is the entire risk.
  • Being wrong. The real one publishes its error bands. This one just tells you the answer.
