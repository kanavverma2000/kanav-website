Every Module, and How It's Computed

Eight analytical layers over seventeen years of AEMO data. Each one below states what it measures, the figure it currently returns, and the condition under which it stops being reliable.

01 — Curtailment attribution
Not how much was lost. Why it was lost.

Curtailment is measured against each unit's unconstrained intermittent generation forecast — what the plant itself told the market it could have produced — and then split by cause. A semi-dispatch cap means the network physically prevented export. Everything else is the plant responding to price.

The split is the product. Network curtailment has been flat near 1 TWh a year since 2021 while economic curtailment rose 420%, which means the growth in curtailment risk is a market-structure problem, not a transmission problem, and it is priced and hedged differently.

Limits. The cause split is attributed over the semi-dispatch-capped subset — 7.60 TWh of the 8.37 TWh total in 2025. The residual is reported as unattributed rather than assigned to either bucket. Behind-the-meter solar is not in scope.

02 — Revenue capture
What each unit earned, against the right denominator.

Capture ratio is generation-weighted price divided by the region's time-weighted average price. That denominator choice is not cosmetic: AEMO publishes both a time-weighted and a volume-weighted series, and the volume-weighted one sits above the time-weighted one by construction. Benchmarking capture against it understates every renewable asset in the market.

Computed per unit at five-minute granularity across 22.3 million records, so the spread within a region is visible — and in Queensland in 2025 that spread was more than eight-fold.

Limits. Capture is a ratio and both halves move. Fleet solar capture "recovered" from 41.9% to 58.7% between H1 2025 and H1 2026 while solar's actual earnings fell from $47.38 to $40.18/MWh — the denominator collapsed faster. Both numbers are always shown together for this reason.

03 — Merit-order counterfactual engine
Re-clear the market twice. Report the difference.

The engine builds a national supply stack from per-unit short-run marginal cost — 169 units priced from AEMO's own published heat rates, station fuel prices and variable operating costs — then clears it against actual demand with and without a chosen unit. The difference is the counterfactual price impact.

It runs under two independent clearing assumptions. Cost-based clearing asks what plant costs to run. Conduct-based clearing replays what participants actually offered, reconstructed from AEMO's bid archive over a 227-unit stack. The gap between the two is the markup component.

Limits, stated in the output. The conduct bound's absolute price level is unvalidated — clearing the unconstrained offer stack gives $43.77 against $94.84 observed, correlation 0.415, because the stack has no network constraints, ramp limits or unit commitment. The counterfactual is a difference between two clears of the same stack so systematic level bias largely cancels, but only the mean is readable, never interval-level impacts, and the result is published as a range.

04 — Coal exit & supply–demand
Closure schedules, and what the stack looks like afterwards.

Tracks the announced retirement schedule across the black and brown coal fleet — 45 units, 21.1 GW — with closure history, station-level context, fleet outlook, interconnector flows and supply–demand balance. Closure impact runs through the counterfactual engine rather than a rule of thumb.

It also carries the diagnostics that make a closure study honest: the midday squeeze, negative-price incidence, high-price event attribution, and the price shape a region actually settles into.

Limits. Announced closure dates are policy statements, not physics, and have moved repeatedly. The module reports the schedule as published and models sensitivity to it — it does not forecast whether a date will hold.

05 — Storage economics
Half of a battery's value is invisible to a spread calculation.

Per-unit charge and discharge, cycling, utilisation, price heatmaps, strategy clustering and offtake structure across the storage fleet. Battery energy is summed from positive intervals at native resolution — monthly net energy is approximately zero because charging cancels discharging, so a monthly aggregate makes the entire fleet disappear.

Valuation runs against the full scarcity-event distribution, not a mean spread. Events run a mean of 7.3 hours and 84.7% of them exceed four hours, so a four-hour battery empties partway through most of the events it was bought for. Net value per MW per year: $287,776 at four hours, $442,879 at eight.

Limits. The eight-hour result survives a capital-cost scenario switch but is no longer described as "the optimum" — switching between two published cost scenarios zeroes an entire 19 GW four-hour fleet, which is more sensitivity than the word implies.

06 — Weather & resource reconstruction
Thirty-six years of weather, so the drought year shows up.

Hourly wind and solar traces reconstructed from reanalysis for 286 projects and 50,168 MW across 36 weather years, 1990 to 2025, calibrated against each unit's unconstrained forecast rather than metered output — metered output is already depressed by curtailment, so calibrating to it bakes the curtailment in.

The point of the long record is the tail. Sampling weather years by mean resource finds the year that breaks the system only by luck: the worst-resource year in the record produces zero unserved energy, and a mid-pack year breaches the reliability standard 25-fold because it contains a deep 72-hour drought.

Limits, and a negative result. Calibration is keyed by region, not by site — five scalars serve 286 projects, and between-site variation runs 0.935–1.331, so a regional factor can be wrong by up to 17 percentage points at an individual site. A higher-resolution Australian reanalysis was tested as a replacement and was worse in every region, so the migration was cancelled rather than shipped.

07 — System model
A five-region build, with the sensitivities published first.

A least-cost capacity expansion across five regions and six interconnectors, with transmission priced inside the optimisation rather than added afterwards as a flat adder, plus pumped hydro, hydrogen storage and a reserve margin. Interconnector limits are derived from AEMO's own dispatch-interval limits cross-checked against eight years of realised flow, not asserted.

The most useful output is the sensitivity ranking, and it is uncomfortable. Moving the discount rate two percentage points changes system cost by 15.6%. Correcting a regional wind calibration by 40% changes it by 0.6%. And switching between two published capital-cost scenarios does not adjust the answer, it transforms it.

The honest headline. The capacity mix is a function of the capital-cost scenario, not a result of the model. Switching scenarios moves solar −41%, wind +61%, and takes a 19 GW four-hour battery fleet to zero with the resource traces untouched. Any capacity outlook that does not name its cost scenario is not telling you anything — ours included.
What actually moves system cost

Reference case $108.65/MWh. Two percentage points of discount rate moves cost 26× more than a 40% error in a regional wind calibration. The demand basis is deliberate and stated: the model answers what it would cost to serve today's demand from a decarbonised fleet — comparing its peaks to a future-year planning standard would be a category error.

08 — Scenarios, price paths & forecast error
An explicit clear with published assumptions. Not a black box.

Forward scenarios are produced by re-clearing the merit-order stack under changed inputs — closure timing, build trajectory, fuel price, demand — with every assumption exposed and versioned. You can see which input moved the answer, which is the entire point of running a scenario rather than reading a forecast.

Alongside it sits a forecast-error layer that decomposes where AEMO's own pre-dispatch forecast goes wrong: by hour, by region, and by whether the interval was a price spike. Spikes account for roughly 99% of five-minute forecast error, which is a structural fact about the supply curve, not a modelling choice — the offer stack is flat, flat, flat, then a cliff.

What is deliberately not sold here. The spike classifier beats a naive "pre-dispatch above $300" rule by 1.1% at an equal alert budget, so it is not presented as a capability and its area-under-curve figure is never quoted. The pre-dispatch bias correction is the result that survives — and its own significance statistic was inflated 3.3× until the error autocorrelation was handled properly. Both figures are reported so the size of the correction stays visible.
