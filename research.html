Q2 2026, $/MWh. 26,208 five-minute dispatch intervals per region. Ten of ten. Year-on-year also matches: the NEM time-weighted average fell $140.18 → $74.12, a $66.06 fall against AEMO's stated −$66.

Why this is not trivial AEMO's headline price is a time-weighted average over five-minute dispatch periods — not demand-weighted. Its separate volume-weighted series weights regional prices by initial supply plus total intermittent generation, not by total demand. Substituting total demand — the obvious guess — runs $1–2/MWh high in every region. That is a visible error, not rounding, and it is the kind of thing that silently invalidates a capture-price benchmark. ARIA computes capture as generation-weighted price divided by the time-weighted denominator, always, because the volume-weighted series sits above the time-weighted one by construction and would understate every renewable asset in the market.
01 — The finding AEMO did not publish
Gas generation fell 30% in a quarter.
It was not the weather. It was six hours.
AEMO attributes the fall to batteries and above-average temperatures, without separating them. Separating them changes what the number means — and what you would contract against it.
+0.5%
Total sent-out energy, Q2 2025 → Q2 2026. Flat. No demand destruction — gas was displaced, not switched off.
69%
Share of the 963 GWh gas fall covered by the 665 GWh rise in battery discharge (+213%).
76%
Of the gas decline sits in six hours, 16:00–21:00. 78% of the battery growth is in the same six hours.
+30 GWh
Midday gas rose, 11:00–14:00, because charging batteries adds load.

The last number is the one that settles it. Weather is a level effect — a mild quarter lowers demand across the day. It cannot cut evening gas by 160 GWh and simultaneously raise midday gas. A displacement mechanism can, and does, because the same asset that removes the evening peak creates a midday trough to charge into.

Gas-powered generation by region. The battery-heavy regions roughly halved. Queensland — next to the LNG trains, and the region with the least storage relative to its peak — held up.

It is a change in shape, not a de-rating of the fleet. The number of gas units running rose from 71 to 76, while unit-hours running fell from 16.0% to 12.0% and average output when on fell from 114.1 MW to 106.0 MW. A running-hours story implies swing and peaking contracts, not baseload — a different commercial question than "gas is in decline".

The honest counter, stated first Q2 2023 gas fell 34% and then rebounded 17% the following year. A 30% fall proves nothing structural on its own. What separates the two is the mechanism: 2023 was a level effect across the day; 2026 is a six-hour displacement plus a midday increase. Also, wind added 1,533 GWh over the same period — the 69% battery coverage is an accounting correspondence, not a causal attribution, and should be quoted that way.
02 — Curtailment attribution
Curtailment grew five-fold in four years.
The grid had almost nothing to do with it.
"Curtailment" is used as though it were one thing. It is two, they have opposite fixes, and only one of them is growing. Network curtailment — a constraint physically preventing export — has been flat at roughly 1 TWh a year since 2021. Economic curtailment — the plant choosing not to generate because the price does not justify it — is up 5.2×.

Total is measured against unconstrained intermittent generation forecast (UIGF) — what the plant told the market it could have produced. This inverts the usual policy conclusion. If curtailment growth were a network problem, transmission build is the fix and every curtailed megawatt-hour is a case for it. It is not. Eighty-five per cent of curtailed energy in 2025 was a price decision, which means the fix is storage, load flexibility, and contract structure — and that a developer's curtailment risk is largely a function of what gets built around them, not what gets built to them.

The regional picture is a solar story. In 2025, South Australia curtailed 39.4% of the utility solar energy that was available to it — 552.6 GWh curtailed against 850.5 GWh delivered. Victoria curtailed 18.9%, New South Wales 18.6%, Queensland 17.4%. New South Wales is the largest absolute loss at 1,920.5 GWh, simply because it has the most solar.

03 — Capture-price divergence
Capture ratio is what a generator actually earned per megawatt-hour, divided by the time-weighted average price in its region. A region-level price forecast implicitly assumes assets in that region earn something close to it. In Queensland in 2025, they did not — the spread inside one region was more than eight-fold.

Coopers Gap and Darling Downs sit in the same market, settle against the same regional reference price, and are about 100 kilometres apart. One beats the market average; the other keeps thirteen cents in the dollar. Nothing in a regional price curve tells you which one you are buying. That gap is the whole argument for per-unit modelling, and it is not a Queensland anomaly — it is the same mechanism everywhere solar is concentrated, showing up hardest where solar penetration ran ahead of storage.

04 — Counterfactual engine
What does closing a coal station do to price?
There are two answers, and the gap between them is the point.
A counterfactual re-clears the market twice — once as it happened, once with a unit added back — and reports the difference. Which answer you get depends entirely on whether you clear the stack at what plant costs to run, or at what it actually offered. ARIA runs both, and they do not agree.

Cost-based bound

Clear the stack at short-run marginal cost, taken per unit from AEMO's own published heat rates, fuel prices and variable O&M. Replay the Liddell closures (450 MW in 2022, 1,350 MW in 2023) into the New South Wales stack.

~$1–2/MWh

Conduct-based bound

Clear the same market at the prices participants actually bid, reconstructed from AEMO's offer archive. Replay the Hazelwood closure (1,238 MW re-inserted) across the full 227-unit national offer stack.

$14–21/MWh

The gap between the two bounds is the markup component — the part of a closure's price impact that comes not from the physical loss of cheap capacity but from how the remaining plant chooses to offer once it is gone. That is a decomposition a single-number forecast cannot give you, and it is why both bounds are published rather than averaged into one.

Where this stops working — read before using either number The two bounds are different closures, in different years, in different regions. They bracket a mechanism; they do not validate each other, and neither should ever be presented as confirming the other. The conduct bound's absolute price level is unvalidated: clearing the unconstrained offer stack gives $43.77 against $94.84 observed, mean absolute error 51, correlation 0.415. The counterfactual is a difference between two clears of the same stack, so systematic level bias largely cancels — but only the mean is readable, not interval-level impacts. It moves $14.00 → $20.59 across treatments, a 32% spread, so it is published as a range and never as a point.

Two things fell out of building this that are worth more than the headline.

A modelled zero is not always a bug. The engine returned no price impact for the Liddell closures at median demand, which looked like a defect for a year. It was not. New South Wales has a genuine cost plateau — eight units priced between $71.98 and $72.73, 4,760 MW derated — and median regional demand sits inside it. Removing 1,350 MW cannot move the marginal cost when the next 4,760 MW cost essentially the same. The impact only bites above roughly 7 GW. Merit-order substitution genuinely explains only $1–2/MWh; the rest is conduct.

Unit ordering was backwards, and the cause was a name. The engine originally ranked coal units by capacity as an efficiency proxy. Reading AEMO's workbook per-unit instead reversed the New South Wales order: Bayswater is the cheapest at $46.49 and Eraring is near the dearest at $72.55, where the capacity proxy had Eraring cheapest. The dispersion is driven by fuel price, which varies 3.2× across stations ($2.06–6.57/GJ), not by unit size — heat rates barely move. In the same pass, Loy Yang A — the largest brown-coal station in the country, 2,210 MW — turned out to have been silently dropped from the brown-coal class average for years, because one AEMO sheet calls it "Loy Yang A" and another calls it "Loy Yang A Power Station". It prices at $19.57/MWh.

05 — Supply-curve structure
The bid stack barely changes.
That is why the market is volatile.
Reconstructing the national offer stack from AEMO's bid archive — 163 months, every fuel — produces a result that sounds contradictory until you look at the shape of the curve.
92.8%
Of unit-days in 2024–26, the price ladder is identical to the previous trading day. Up from 89.4% pre-2021.
98.6%
Of consecutive five-minute interval pairs have unchanged band volumes.
$0
Median price step between adjacent used bands within a unit-day. The 90th percentile is $55.
$15,795
Maximum step between adjacent used bands. Flat, flat, flat — cliff.

A near-static supply curve does not imply stable prices. It implies the opposite, once the curve is non-linear enough. Roughly 22.5 GW is offered at or below zero dollars, and national demand sits right at the top of that must-run block — so all price formation happens in a thin, steep middle. Volatility is demand sliding across a nearly-discontinuous curve, and that is the same mechanism that makes spikes account for the overwhelming majority of five-minute forecast error.

And the cliff is getting steeper. Comparing the capped, cleaned offer curve in 2009 against 2026:

The bridge between the sub-zero block and the scarcity tail has thinned from 12,785 MW to 9,512 MW in absolute terms while demand grew — a bigger floor, a thinner bridge, a bigger tail.

Price history tells you where you landed. Only the offer stack tells you the shape of the curve you landed on. That is the argument for reconstructing bids, and it cannot be obtained any other way.

Reconstruction is where this goes wrong The bid data is public and the edge is not access — it is rebuilding it correctly. The sum of a unit's band volumes is not its offered capacity: 82% of rows over-offer, and uncapped the fleet appears to offer 2,000 MW against a 1,600 MW nameplate. Capping at maximum availability moved one published Hazelwood figure by more than a third. AEMO's settlement date is a trading day running 04:05 to 04:00, so a naive join silently mixes months. And a first run of the curve-shape analysis that bypassed these guards reported 101,594 MW offered below zero in 2018 — more than the entire installed capacity of the market. Every figure above comes from the guarded path.
06 — Weather, adequacy and system cost
The worst year for reliability
is not the worst year for weather.
ARIA rebuilds hourly wind and solar traces for 36 weather years, 1990 to 2025, across every region, and runs a capacity-expansion model against them with plant outages drawn stochastically — 3,000 realisations.

Three of ten tested weather years breach the market's 0.002% unserved-energy standard. Which three is the finding. 1992 has the worst mean resource in the entire 36-year ensemble and returns zero unserved energy. 2009 is mid-pack on annual average and breaches the standard by 25× — because it contains the third-deepest 72-hour wind-and-solar drought in the record.

Adequacy is decided by the worst coincidence, not the worst average. A planning study that samples weather years by mean resource will find the dangerous year only by luck. Sampling has to target the drought tail explicitly, and the model now does.

The same ensemble sizes storage honestly. Scarcity events run a mean of 7.3 hours and a median of 6.0, and 84.7% of them last longer than four hours — so a four-hour battery empties partway through most of the events it was bought for. Valued against the full event distribution rather than a mean arbitrage spread, net value per MW per year runs $287,776 at four hours and $442,879 at eight. At eight hours only about half that value is arbitrage, which is why a spread-based valuation cannot see it.

What sensitivity testing did to our own conclusions Running the system model against varied inputs produced an uncomfortable ranking. Moving the discount rate by two percentage points changes system cost by −15.6%. Correcting a regional wind calibration by 40% changes it by 0.6% — twenty-six times less. And switching between two published capital-cost scenarios does not move the answer, it transforms it: solar built falls 41%, wind rises 61%, and an entire 19 GW four-hour battery fleet goes to zero, with the resource traces untouched. The capacity mix is a function of the capital-cost scenario, not a result of the model. Any capacity outlook that does not say which cost scenario produced it is not telling you anything. Our own "eight-hour battery is optimal" finding survives the switch, but "optimal" implies a robustness that a result sitting next to a zeroed-out 19 GW fleet has not earned, and it is no longer stated that way.
07 — Negative results
Things we tested, that failed,
and publish anyway.
A method is only worth as much as the tests it survived. These are the ones it did not, each of which changed what the platform does.
  • A better weather dataset turned out to be worse. The Bureau of Meteorology's BARRA reanalysis is higher-resolution and Australia-specific, so it should beat the global ERA5 product for solar. Measured across 116 operating sites, 18,677 MW and 3.05 million site-hours, it does not: capacity-factor bias against the unconstrained forecast is +35.3% for BARRA against +28.7% for ERA5 nationally, worse in every region. On the lowest decile of daily fleet output — the drought tail the whole exercise existed to probe — ERA5 is better again, +34.4% against +46.9%. The migration was cancelled. It cost one day, which is what the gate was for.
  • Our own spike classifier is not a product. It was reported internally at 0.998 area-under-curve, which is meaningless at a 0.37% event rate. Held to the same alert budget as the naive rule "the five-minute pre-dispatch forecast is above $300", it wins by 1.1%. It is not sold as a differentiator and the AUC figure is never quoted. The pre-dispatch bias correction underneath it is the result that stands up — and its own significance statistic was inflated 3.3× until the error autocorrelation was handled properly.
  • The leading explanation for our clearing error was wrong. The conduct-based stack offers 38,707 MW against 21,970 MW actually generated, and hydro is much the largest block of undelivered offer — 6,541 MW offered against 925 delivered, held back by water value rather than price. Removing hydro from the price-setting stack should have fixed the level. It made it worse: mean absolute error 51.24 → 56.18, correlation 0.415 → 0.357. Network constraints remain the leading untested cause, and saying so is more useful than shipping a fix that does not work.
  • The registry's per-unit solar capacity cannot be used as a denominator. One unit is registered at 2,421 MW and never forecasts above 29 MW — an 83× error. Three others are out by 76×, 69× and 56×. Using registry capacity produced capacity factors above 1.0, which is physically impossible, and drove one region's apparent bias to +3,396%. The registry disagrees by more than 1.5× on 52 of 86 site-years, so anything computing per-unit solar capacity factor from it is suspect. The fix is to derive nameplate from the 99.5th percentile of the unit's own unconstrained forecast.
  • The obvious validation target is not trustworthy. A difference-in-differences estimate of the Liddell closures — New South Wales against Queensland as a control — is non-monotonic in capacity removed: it reports a smaller price effect for 1,350 MW than for 450 MW. It is dominated by the 2022 gas crisis. The engine is monotonic; the observational estimate is not, so the engine is not calibrated to it. "The model brackets the observation" was also rejected as a pass — a range of $0.61 to $41.96 cannot fail.
  • A published figure of ours was never produced by the model. A curtailment projection quoted at 84% for one renewable energy zone came from a hard-coded array in a chart-patching script, not from the model, which said 78.4%. All seven deviations ran the same direction. The standing rule since: a number read off a rendered chart is not a number from the model, and nothing is published without re-deriving it from the file the model writes.
Scope
What this is, and what it isn't.

Built and serving

  • Per-unit curtailment with an economic-versus-network cause split, 217 semi-scheduled units
  • Merit-order counterfactual engine — per-unit short-run marginal cost from AEMO's published workbook, 169 units
  • Conduct-based closure replay over a reconstructed 227-unit national offer stack
  • Per-unit revenue capture at five-minute granularity, 22.3 million records
  • 36 weather years of reconstructed hourly wind and solar, 286 projects, 50,168 MW
  • Five-region capacity-expansion model with transmission priced inside the optimisation
  • Seventeen years of price history, 2009 to today, five-minute and hourly
  • Quantile price-path scenarios with pre-dispatch bias correction

Not claimed

  • No forward marginal-loss-factor forecasting. A prototype exists; it is unwired, runs on 16 hard-coded units, and is not a capability
  • No AI price forecasting. The scenario engine is an explicit merit-order clear with published assumptions, not a learned black box
  • No spike-prediction edge — measured at 1.1% over a naive rule
  • No automated bidding. Running an optimiser and benchmarking optimisers are a conflict of interest
  • No constraint-level dollar attribution — that needs a full network solver, which this is not
  • No solar coverage in the second-opinion weather product, and its wind coverage is 68 of 130 farms
  • Interval-level accuracy on the conduct-based clear. Only the mean is readable

Data basis. Hourly generation 2019-12 to 2026-06, 24.47 million records. Prices 2009-07 to 2026-08, five-minute and hourly. Revenue capture 22.34 million records.
