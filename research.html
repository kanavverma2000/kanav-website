Why it isn't trivial. AEMO's headline is a time-weighted average over five-minute dispatch periods. Its volume-weighted series is weighted by initial supply plus total intermittent generation, not total demand. Use total demand, the obvious guess, and you run $1–2/MWh high in every region.

01 · Gas
+30 GWh
Midday gas rose while evening gas collapsed.
Weather is a level effect. It cannot do both. So this is displacement, not a mild quarter.
Show the working

Gas fell 30% over the year. AEMO reports the evening concentration and names batteries, and I reproduce that: my battery discharge increase over their exact 16:00–21:00 window is 479.2 GWh against their stated 485.0, a 1.2% gap.

What is not in their report is the midday rise. Battery charging adds load, so gas output between 11:00 and 14:00 went up 30 GWh while the evening fell 160. A mild quarter lowers demand across the whole day; it cannot do both.

Total sent-out energy was flat at +0.5%, so nothing was destroyed, it moved. Substitution runs one-for-one at 16:00 and 17:00, and battery growth covers 69% of the 963 GWh fall. That last figure is an accounting correspondence, not a causal attribution, and should be quoted that way.

If the growth were a network problem, transmission is the fix. It isn't. The fix is storage, load flexibility and contract structure, and your exposure is mostly to what gets built around you rather than to you.

03 · Capture
8×
The best and worst assets in the NEM share a price region.
Coopers Gap captured 106.6% of the Queensland average. Darling Downs, 12.9%.
Show the working

Capture ratio is what a generator earned per megawatt-hour over the time-weighted average price in its region. A regional forecast assumes assets there earn something close to it. In Queensland in 2025 they did not.

Same market, same reference price, about 100 km apart. One beats the market average; the other keeps thirteen cents in the dollar. Nothing in a regional curve tells you which one you bought. Fleet medians: wind 72.1%, solar 39.8%, across 125 assets that generated more than 150 GWh.

04 · Counterfactuals
$1 or $20
A coal closure is worth both, per MWh.
Clear at cost, or clear at what was actually bid. The gap between them is market power.
Show the working

A counterfactual clears the market twice, once as it happened and once with the unit added back, and reports the difference. Which answer you get depends entirely on whether you clear at what plant costs or at what it actually offered.

Cost-based, replaying the Liddell closures into the NSW stack: ~$1–2/MWh. Conduct-based, replaying Hazelwood across the 227-unit national offer stack: $14–21/MWh. The difference separates physical substitution from bidding conduct.

Where it stops working. Different closures, different years, different regions. They bracket a mechanism; neither validates the other. The conduct bound's absolute level is unvalidated: clearing the unconstrained stack gives $43.77 against $94.84 observed, correlation 0.415. Only the mean is readable, so it is published as a range, never a point.

A modelled zero is not always a bug. NSW has a genuine cost plateau, eight units between $71.98 and $72.73 across 4,760 MW derated, and median demand sits inside it. Removing 1,350 MW cannot move the marginal cost. Reading AEMO's workbook per unit also reversed the NSW order: Bayswater cheapest at $46.49, Eraring near the dearest at $72.55, where the capacity proxy had Eraring cheapest.

05 · Supply curve
92.8%
The bid stack barely moves. That is why prices swing.
Median step between used bands is $0. The maximum is $15,795.
Show the working

Ladders are identical to the previous trading day in 92.8% of unit-days and band volumes are unchanged in 98.6% of consecutive intervals. A near-static curve doesn't imply stable prices; once it is non-linear enough it implies the opposite.

About 22.5 GW is offered at or below zero and national demand sits right at the top of that must-run block, so all price formation happens in a thin, steep middle. Volatility is demand sliding across a nearly-discontinuous curve, which is the same mechanism that makes spikes roughly 99% of five-minute forecast error.

And the cliff is steepening. Since 2009 the must-run floor more than doubled while the cheap-but-positive middle collapsed 61%. The bridge between the sub-zero block and the scarcity tail thinned from 12,785 MW to 9,512 MW while demand grew.

Reconstruction is where this goes wrong. The sum of a unit's band volumes is not its offered capacity: 82% of rows over-offer, and uncapped the fleet appears to offer 2,000 MW against a 1,600 MW nameplate. A first run that bypassed those guards reported 101,594 MW offered below zero in 2018, more than the entire installed capacity of the market.

06 · Adequacy
25×
The worst weather year caused no blackouts.
1992 has the worst mean resource in 36 years and returns zero unserved energy.
Show the working

I rebuild hourly wind and solar for 36 weather years across every region, then run a capacity-expansion model with outages drawn stochastically, 3,000 realisations.

Three of ten tested years breach the 0.002% unserved-energy standard. Which three is the finding. 2009 is mid-pack on annual average and breaches by 25 times, because it holds the third-deepest 72-hour drought in the record. Adequacy is decided by the worst coincidence, not the worst average. Sample by mean resource and you find the dangerous year only by luck.

The same ensemble sizes storage honestly. Events run a mean of 7.3 hours and 84.7% exceed four, so a four-hour battery empties partway through most of what it was bought for. Net value runs $287,776/MW/yr at four hours and $442,879 at eight, and at eight only about half is arbitrage.

What sensitivity testing did to my own conclusions. Switching between two published capital-cost scenarios does not adjust the answer, it transforms it: solar −41%, wind +61%, and a 19 GW four-hour battery fleet to zero, resource traces untouched. The capacity mix is a function of the capex scenario, not a result of the model. Mine included.

Scope

What this is, and what it isn't.

Data basis and limits

Hourly generation 2019-12 to 2026-06, 24,472,554 records. Prices 2009-07 to 2026-08. Revenue capture 22,342,213 records. 562 registered units. Fuel keys on per-unit registry fuel, never the station technology tag, because hybrid sites otherwise drop wind and solar into the battery bucket, which overstated one month's battery discharge by 29%.

Not claimed: no forward loss-factor forecasting, no AI price forecasting, no spike-prediction edge, no automated bidding, no constraint-level dollar attribution, no interval-level accuracy on the conduct clear.
